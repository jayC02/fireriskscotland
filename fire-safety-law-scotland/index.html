Fire safety law in Scotland: a guide for duty holders

Scotland has its own fire safety legislation, separate from England and Wales. This guide explains who has duties, which premises are covered, what must be written down, how the law is enforced, and what is changing.

By Raj Chall BSc, IFE-registered fire risk assessor. Updated 12 November 2026.

In short: fire safety in most non-domestic premises in Scotland is governed by Part 3 of the Fire (Scotland) Act 2005 and the Fire Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006, which came into force on 1 October 2006. Employers and anyone with control of "relevant premises" must assess the risk from fire, take the fire safety measures needed, and keep the assessment under review. The Scottish Fire and Rescue Service enforces the law and can serve enforcement and prohibition notices. The most serious offences carry a fine of up to £20,000 on summary conviction, or an unlimited fine and up to two years' imprisonment on indictment.

The legislation

Who is a duty holder

Scottish law uses the term duty holder. The Act places duties on:

  • Employers

    Section 53: ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the fire safety of employees.

  • People with control

    Section 54: anyone with control of relevant premises, to the extent of that control, must ensure the fire safety of other relevant persons.

  • Employees

    Must take reasonable care for their own and others' fire safety, and co-operate with their employer.

Several people can be duty holders for the same building: an employer occupying an office, the landlord responsible for the common stair and the managing agent running the building. They must co-operate and co-ordinate their measures. Bringing in an outside fire risk assessor does not move the duty. As the Scottish Fire and Rescue Service puts it, even where external help is used, the duty holder remains legally responsible and accountable.

Which premises are covered

Part 3 applies to relevant premises, defined in section 78 of the Act. That covers most non-domestic premises, including:

  • Workplaces

    Offices, shops, factories, warehouses and other places of work.

  • Sleeping accommodation

    Hotels, guest houses, hostels, holiday lets and student halls.

  • Licensed HMOs

    Excluded from the definition of domestic premises once within HMO licensing.

  • Care premises

    Premises providing a care home service, even in a domestic-style building.

  • Public buildings

    Schools, churches, community halls, leisure centres and similar.

  • Childminding

    Dwellings used for childminding, for the time they are used for that purpose.

Private homes are generally excluded. Fire safety in homes is dealt with mainly through the Tolerable Standard, the Repairing Standard for private landlords, and building regulations.

The duty to assess

The core duty is to carry out an assessment of the risk from fire and to identify the fire safety measures needed. The Scottish Fire and Rescue Service describes five steps:

  1. Identify the people at risk

    Everyone in or near the premises, and especially those at greater risk.

  2. Identify fire hazards

    Sources of ignition, fuel and oxygen, and how they could come together.

  3. Evaluate the risk and decide whether measures are adequate

    Remove or reduce hazards, and protect people with fire safety measures, giving preference to collective measures over individual ones.

  4. Record the information

    Where the law requires it, record the significant findings and the measures taken or planned.

  5. Review

    Keep the assessment under review and update it when things change.

When the assessment must be written down

Regulation 8 of the Fire Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006 requires the duty holder, as soon as practicable after an assessment is carried out or reviewed, to record specified information where:

  • Five or more employees

    The duty holder employs five or more people.

  • Licence or registration

    A licence or registration under an enactment is required for the premises, as with HMOs, short-term lets, licensed venues and care homes.

  • Alterations notice

    An alterations notice requiring a record is in force.

Regulation 9 sets out what must be recorded: the significant findings of the assessment, including the measures that have been or will be taken, and any person or group identified as being especially at risk. Regulation 10 separately requires fire safety arrangements to be recorded in the same circumstances.

When to review

Regulation 3 requires a review to be carried out regularly so the assessment stays up to date, and in particular if there is reason to suspect it is no longer valid, or there has been a significant change in the matters it relates to. The Scottish Government's guidance adds that reviews should also follow a fire or near miss. Most duty holders review at least once a year.

Enforcement and penalties

The Scottish Fire and Rescue Service is the main enforcing authority. Enforcement officers can inspect premises, ask to see the assessment and records, and give informal advice. For more serious failings they can serve notices:

NoticeEffect
Enforcement noticeRequires specified steps to be taken to remedy a failure to comply with the duties, within a set period.
Prohibition noticeProhibits or restricts the use of premises where the risk to people in a fire is so serious that use should be restricted. Can take immediate effect.
Alterations noticeRequires the enforcing authority to be told before certain changes are made to premises that pose serious risk.

Section 72 creates the offences. Failing to comply with the fire safety duties is an offence where the failure puts a relevant person at risk of death or serious injury in the event of fire. Failing to comply with an enforcement, prohibition or alterations notice is also an offence. For the most serious offences, the maximum penalty is:

£20,000maximum fine on summary conviction
Unlimitedfine on conviction on indictment
2 yearsmaximum imprisonment on indictment, with or without a fine

Directors, managers and similar officers of a company can also be personally liable where an offence by the company was committed with their consent or connivance, or was attributable to their neglect.

How Scotland differs from England and Wales

ScotlandEngland and Wales
Main lawFire (Scotland) Act 2005, Part 3, and Fire Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005
Person responsibleDuty holderResponsible person
EnforcerScottish Fire and Rescue ServiceLocal fire and rescue authority
Blocks of flatsDomestic premises generally excluded; guidance for high-rise buildingsFire Safety Act 2021 confirmed structure, external walls and flat entrance doors are covered
Home smoke alarmsInterlinked smoke and heat alarms in every home since February 2022Requirements for rented homes; no equivalent universal rule

Guidance, templates and fire risk assessments written for English law can still be useful, but a report that cites the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order as the governing law is citing the wrong legislation for Scottish premises.

Other rules that affect fire safety

Automatic fire alarms

Since 1 July 2023, the Scottish Fire and Rescue Service no longer attends automatic fire alarm calls from commercial and workplace premises unless a fire is confirmed. Premises with sleeping accommodation continue to receive an emergency response. Duty holders need a safe procedure for investigating alarms and deciding when to call 999.

Interlinked alarms in homes

Since 1 February 2022, every home in Scotland must have a smoke alarm in the room used most during the day, a smoke alarm in every circulation space on each storey, a heat alarm in every kitchen, all ceiling-mounted and interlinked, plus a carbon monoxide alarm where there is a carbon-fuelled appliance or flue. Private landlords must meet this as part of the Repairing Standard.

What is changing

In its response to the Grenfell Tower Inquiry, the Scottish Government committed to strengthen fire risk assessor competency in Scotland and the requirement on duty holders to hire a competent assessor, through legislation following public consultation. It also committed to further consultation on mandatory periodic fire risk assessments in specified high-rise buildings. At present there is no legal requirement in Scotland for a fire risk assessor to hold formal qualifications, which is why choosing a registered assessor matters.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main fire safety law in Scotland?

Part 3 of the Fire (Scotland) Act 2005, supported by the Fire Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006. Together they set out fire safety duties for most non-domestic premises in Scotland.

Does the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 apply in Scotland?

No. The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 applies in England and Wales. Scotland has its own legislation: the Fire (Scotland) Act 2005 and the Fire Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006.

What is a duty holder in Scottish fire law?

A duty holder is a person with fire safety duties under Part 3 of the Fire (Scotland) Act 2005. That includes employers, in respect of their employees, and anyone who has control of relevant premises to any extent, in respect of other people on or near the premises. It is the Scottish equivalent of the "responsible person" in England and Wales.

What are the penalties for breaching fire safety law in Scotland?

For the most serious offences under section 72 of the Fire (Scotland) Act 2005, the maximum penalty is a fine of up to £20,000 on summary conviction, or an unlimited fine and up to two years' imprisonment on conviction on indictment. These offences apply where a failure puts a relevant person at risk of death or serious injury in the event of fire, or where a notice is not complied with.

Do I need a qualified person to do my fire risk assessment in Scotland?

Not by law at present, although the Scottish Government has committed to legislate on assessor competency. The Scottish Fire and Rescue Service advises duty holders who use an outside assessor to check their competence, for example by choosing someone on a professional register or certified by a UKAS-accredited body.
